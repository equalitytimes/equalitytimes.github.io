Biases of Artificial Intelligence in Discrimination: A Legal and Ethical Challenge

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being used to assist or influence decisions that have significant consequences for individuals. Employers use automated systems to screen applicants, financial institutions use algorithms to assess borrowers, housing providers use automated tools to evaluate prospective tenants, and digital platforms use algorithmic systems to determine which advertisements and opportunities are presented to users.

These technologies can improve efficiency and allow institutions to process large amounts of information. At the same time, their increasing role in decision-making raises an important legal question: what happens when an automated system produces discriminatory outcomes?

AI systems are not inherently neutral. They are developed by people, trained on data generated within existing social and institutional structures, and designed according to objectives selected by their developers or users. Where historical data reflects discrimination or unequal access to opportunities, an automated system may reproduce those patterns. Bias may also arise from the variables selected, the objectives assigned to a system, or the way its outputs are interpreted.

Algorithmic discrimination therefore presents a challenge for existing anti-discrimination law. The use of technology does not, by itself, remove legal obligations that would apply to a comparable human decision. However, automated decision-making can make discriminatory practices more difficult to identify, explain and challenge. Addressing this problem requires a combination of legal accountability, appropriate testing, transparency and meaningful human oversight.

How Bias Enters Artificial Intelligence

Bias can enter an AI system at several stages of its development and use.

One significant source is training data. Machine-learning systems identify statistical relationships from information supplied to them. If that information reflects historical discrimination or unequal access to education, employment, housing, credit or other opportunities, a system may learn patterns associated with those inequalities.

For example, an employer may develop a recruitment model using information about employees previously regarded as successful. If historical recruitment practices disproportionately favoured a particular demographic group, the system could identify characteristics associated with that group as indicators of a desirable candidate. Removing race or sex from the dataset would not necessarily eliminate the problem if other variables operate as proxies for protected characteristics.

Bias can also result from the objectives established for an algorithm. A system designed primarily to maximise efficiency, reduce costs or predict previous outcomes does not automatically account for equality or fairness. Decisions concerning which variables should be considered, how success should be measured and what degree of error is acceptable are ultimately human decisions.

The problem, therefore, is not simply that an algorithm can be described as "biased." A more fundamental concern is that a mathematical process that appears neutral may reproduce existing inequalities while giving the resulting decision an appearance of objectivity.

AI and Employment Discrimination

Employment is one of the most significant areas in which algorithmic decision-making can affect individual rights. Employers may use automated systems to advertise vacancies, screen résumés, rank applicants, evaluate assessments, analyse interviews or recommend candidates.

In the United States, Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits employment discrimination on specified grounds, including race, colour, religion, sex and national origin. The Americans with Disabilities Act also provides protections against disability discrimination.

The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) has recognised that employers' use of artificial intelligence and algorithmic technologies can raise concerns under existing employment-discrimination laws.

Consider an automated assessment system that systematically disadvantages applicants with certain disabilities because of the way the assessment measures communication, movement or interaction with software. The fact that an algorithm produced the result does not, by itself, resolve the legal question. The relevant issue remains whether the employment practice complies with applicable anti-discrimination requirements.

This illustrates a broader principle: automation changes the mechanism through which a decision is made, but it does not automatically remove the legal responsibilities associated with that decision.

Algorithmic Discrimination in Housing

Housing is another area in which algorithmic systems can have significant consequences. Landlords and property managers may use automated tenant-screening systems that analyse credit histories, rental records, debts and other information.

The application of the Fair Housing Act to algorithmic decision-making has received increasing legal attention. In Louis et al. v. SafeRent et al., plaintiffs challenged an algorithm-based tenant-screening system, alleging that the system disproportionately disadvantaged Black and Hispanic applicants. The U.S. Department of Justice filed a Statement of Interest addressing the application of the Fair Housing Act to algorithmic tenant screening.

Another significant case involved Meta Platforms. In 2022, the U.S. Department of Justice alleged that Meta's housing advertising system discriminated against Facebook users on characteristics protected under the Fair Housing Act. The case resulted in a settlement requiring changes to Meta's housing-advertising system and measures intended to address disparities in the delivery of personalised advertisements.

These cases demonstrate that algorithmic discrimination can occur even before an individual formally applies for housing. If an automated advertising system determines who is likely to see a housing opportunity, some individuals may effectively be excluded from an opportunity without knowing that it was available to them.

AI in Credit and Lending

Artificial intelligence is also being used in financial decision-making. Lenders may employ increasingly sophisticated models to assess creditworthiness and make lending decisions.

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) prohibits certain forms of discrimination in credit transactions, while Regulation B establishes requirements implementing the Act. The increasing complexity of automated credit models has raised additional questions about transparency and the ability of individuals to understand adverse decisions.

One important concern is the so-called "black-box" problem. A complex model may identify statistical relationships that are difficult for an individual applicant, and sometimes even an institution, to understand.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has addressed this issue in relation to adverse-action notices. Its guidance states that creditors using complex algorithms must still provide specific and accurate reasons for adverse credit decisions where such explanations are legally required. The complexity of the technology does not eliminate the applicable legal obligation.

This principle is important for accountability. A person denied credit should not lose the protections provided by law simply because an institution has incorporated an automated system into its decision-making process.

Transparency and Accountability

One of the central legal challenges associated with AI is determining responsibility.

An automated decision may involve several actors: a developer who designs a model, a company that trains it, a technology provider that supplies the system, and an employer, landlord or financial institution that ultimately relies upon its output. When discriminatory consequences occur, identifying responsibility may therefore become more complicated.

There is also an information imbalance between institutions and individuals. A rejected applicant may receive only a general explanation. A prospective tenant may not know how an automated score was calculated. A borrower may be told that a model determined the outcome without being given meaningful information about the factors that influenced the decision.

Transparency does not necessarily require organisations to publish their complete source code or proprietary systems. Meaningful transparency may instead involve informing individuals when automated decision-making is being used, documenting important factors influencing decisions, maintaining mechanisms for correcting inaccurate information, and conducting appropriate assessments of potential discriminatory effects.

The ability to challenge a decision is equally important. Where an automated decision has significant consequences, individuals should have a realistic opportunity to seek review and correction rather than being left with an unexplained computer-generated outcome.

Preventing Algorithmic Discrimination

Addressing algorithmic discrimination requires more than improving the technical accuracy of AI systems. Organisations should also evaluate whether systems produce materially different outcomes or error rates for different groups.

Bias assessments should take place before deployment and continue after a system is introduced. Monitoring is particularly important because the data, users and circumstances surrounding an AI system may change over time.

Human oversight is another important safeguard. However, meaningful human oversight requires more than placing a person somewhere in the decision-making process. Reviewers should have sufficient information, authority and training to question an automated recommendation and, where appropriate, reject or modify it.

Organisations should also maintain appropriate records concerning the development and operation of high-impact systems. Documentation can include the purpose of the system, the categories of data used, known limitations, testing procedures and mechanisms for monitoring discriminatory outcomes.

Where automated systems are used in areas such as employment, housing, credit, education or other significant opportunities, individuals should also have accessible mechanisms to identify errors, seek explanations and challenge decisions where applicable.

The role of public authorities is equally important. In April 2023, the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, Department of Justice, Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and Federal Trade Commission issued a joint statement emphasising that existing laws against discrimination and unfair practices apply to automated systems. The statement reflected a broader regulatory position: technological innovation does not place decision-making outside the reach of existing legal protections.

The Legal Challenge Ahead

Artificial intelligence has considerable potential to improve decision-making and expand access to services. But technological efficiency should not be treated as synonymous with neutrality.

AI systems are created by humans, trained using human-generated information and deployed within societies that already contain economic and social inequalities. Consequently, a system may reproduce discriminatory patterns even when discrimination was not its stated objective.

This creates a difficult question for modern law. Existing anti-discrimination frameworks were largely developed before contemporary AI systems became widely used. Yet their underlying principles remain relevant when automated technologies influence consequential decisions.

The challenge is therefore not necessarily to prevent the use of AI. It is to ensure that technological development remains compatible with equality, accountability and individual rights.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is changing the way institutions make and implement decisions. As these systems become more influential, questions of algorithmic fairness will increasingly become questions of legal responsibility.

The fundamental principle should remain clear: the use of an algorithm should not provide an escape from legal obligations that would apply to a comparable decision made by a human being.

Effective safeguards require appropriate testing, transparency, meaningful human oversight, mechanisms for correcting inaccurate information and enforcement of applicable anti-discrimination laws.

The future of responsible AI will therefore depend not only on how accurately machines can process information, but also on whether the institutions that deploy them remain accountable for the consequences of their decisions.
