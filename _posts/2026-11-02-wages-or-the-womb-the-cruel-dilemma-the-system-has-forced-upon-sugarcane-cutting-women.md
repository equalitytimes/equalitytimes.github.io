The story of Maharashtra's sugar industry is usually told through factory chimneys, crushing-season figures and the price of sugar. Behind that sweetness, however, lies the bitter side of the lives of lakhs of men and women who step into the fields with a sickle in hand. A recent incident from Jalna district is proof of this. A total of 107 sugarcane-cutting women, 63 from Ghansawangi taluka and 44 from Partur taluka, had their wombs removed so that period pains would not cost them their wages. These surgeries are said to have taken place over fourteen months, from 1 August 2025 to 25 October 2026. An angry question is now being asked: how did the health department get no wind of this while it was happening in village after village? The question is valid, because this is not an incident confined to one family or one village. It is the failure of an entire system, and a shameful one.

To understand these women's decision, one must look at the economics of sugarcane cutting. Even before the season begins, the mukadam, the labour contractor, gives a working couple an advance. Sometimes it is a few thousand rupees; sometimes it runs to a lakh or so. This advance is no help at all; it is a chain that binds the worker for the entire season. Husband and wife are counted as a single "jodi", a unit, and together they must cut a fixed tonnage every day. If the wife does not go to work for a day, the unit remains incomplete, and the shortfall is deducted from the wages. A couple is expected to cut two to three tonnes of cane daily. In such circumstances, the four or five days of menstruation are no longer just days of physical suffering. They become days that wipe out income and add to the burden of debt.

Conditions in the fields make this ordeal worse. The workers camp near the cane fields or on factory premises, in tents or makeshift huts. There are no bathing facilities or toilets there, and when her period arrives, a woman's difficulties multiply. A shortage of drinking water, difficulty in obtaining sanitary pads, fourteen to fifteen hours of work a day, and a bundle of cane weighing twenty to forty kilograms on the head: all of this has to be endured even while cramps tear at the abdomen. The women who had their wombs removed because of abdominal pain say that eight to ten days of wages were being lost during their periods. To avoid that loss and to be free of the pain, they had the surgery done in private hospitals, on the advice of private doctors. In that sentence, the women are describing their own choice. But the question remains: how free was this choice? If, caught between starvation and debt, only one door appears open, then walking through that door cannot truly be called a choice.

The Jalna incident is not new. It is the next link in a tragic chain that has been running for years in Beed district. It is estimated that around ten lakh sugarcane workers migrate from Marathwada every year, and nearly half of them are from Beed. In 2019, the issue first drew statewide attention. According to a state government report that year, of roughly 82,300 sugarcane-cutting women in Beed, more than 13,800, nearly sixteen percent, had had their wombs removed over the previous ten years. Many of them were in the thirty-to-thirty-five age group, that is, in the prime of life. The National Commission for Women then sent a notice to the Chief Secretary of Maharashtra. The matter was debated in the Legislative Council. The then health minister announced that the permission of the District Surgeon would be made mandatory before a womb could be removed. In the years that followed, more than six hundred further surgeries came to light. More recently, in the Legislative Council, MLC Uma Khapre called the government to account, stating that about 843 women in Beed had had their wombs removed between 2019 and 2025. The High Court has also instructed that pregnant women should not be sent for cane cutting. After so much discussion, so many committees and so many announcements, if the names of 107 women are surfacing in Jalna today, one can see how deep the gap is between declarations and actual implementation.

We must examine who benefits most from this gap. Many scholars and journalists have pointed to the role of private hospitals. Rural health workers say that uneducated women who come with complaints of abdominal pain, back pain or occasional bleeding are frightened with talk of cancer and advised to have the womb removed. The head of one health organisation has observed that doctors link every complaint to the uterus. As a result, the women do not get treatment for the underlying illness, and new health problems dog them instead. Some women again take an advance from the mukadam to pay for the surgery. In other words, a surgery undertaken to save wages pushes them deeper into debt. This double burden on worker families serves the interests of both the contractors and the private healthcare system.

Does the women's life improve after the surgery? Most of the time, it does not. If the womb is removed at a young age, and the ovaries are removed along with it, early menopause sets in. The consequences include hormonal imbalance, brittle bones, a heightened risk of heart disease, weight gain, joint pain, irritability and depression. The woman who had the surgery to be free of pain finds that back pain and fatigue make work in the fields even harder. For lack of money, she cannot get treatment again. Some women in Beed have said exactly this while recounting their own experiences: they had the surgery, but the pain did not stop and their health kept deteriorating. Today they are telling their daughters and daughters-in-law not to choose this path. This testimony is as important as government reports.

This is not a problem of Marathwada alone; it is a problem of the whole country. According to the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5), about three percent of women aged fifteen to forty-nine in the country have had their wombs removed. But the picture differs greatly from state to state. The proportion is about nine percent in Andhra Pradesh and about eight percent in Telangana, whereas in the north-eastern states it is around one percent. It is no coincidence that the proportion appears higher in regions with migrant labour such as sugarcane cutting and a network of private health services. About seventy percent of the women in the survey who had their wombs removed had the surgery in private hospitals. There are also allegations that unnecessary surgeries are performed to claim money from government health insurance schemes. Dr. Narendra Gupta filed a public interest petition in the Supreme Court alleging that such practices had occurred in Bihar, Rajasthan and Chhattisgarh. In 2023, the court directed all states to implement the Central government's guidelines within three months. Under these guidelines, every district is to have a monitoring committee; private and government hospitals are to submit a list of women who have had their wombs removed every month; the reasons for surgeries on women under forty are to be recorded; and alternative treatments are to be suggested first. In Karnataka too, in 2015, a case came to light in which the wombs of about 2,200 women from marginalised communities had been removed, and four hospitals were involved.

A comparison noted by the court gives pause for thought. In developed countries, hysterectomy is usually performed on women nearing menopause, that is, those above forty-five. In India, however, studies show that it has risen even among young women aged twenty-eight to thirty-six. In the medical world, this surgery is considered a last resort. The accepted norm is that it should be considered only after medicines, hormonal treatment or less invasive methods have failed. Here, though, it is being used as a "quick fix" for financial hardship. Unless we understand that this is not a problem of health but of labour exploitation, we will not find the answer.

The subject has also drawn attention at the international level. The story of the surgeries on sugarcane-cutting women in Beed was presented to the world by media such as Britain's The Guardian, and a social media post by a French outlet based on it was viewed by several crore people. Climate Home News travelled through the sugarcane belts of Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh to report on the condition of these women, highlighting the link between this migration and climate change, erratic rainfall and drought. Farming stands idle for eight to nine months and there is no alternative employment, so families turn to cane cutting. When temperatures rise by six or seven degrees, the health of a woman wielding a sickle for ten to twelve hours in the sun is endangered even further. A 2020 study by researchers at Symbiosis University in Pune concluded that the living and working conditions of these women violate basic human rights. India has signed the United Nations convention on the elimination of all forms of discrimination against women, CEDAW. Under it, the state is obliged to ensure that women get safe working conditions and healthcare. The International Labour Organization's concept of decent work also includes a safe environment, fair remuneration and non-discriminatory treatment. How the sugarcane fields measure up against these tests is a question that could embarrass India, a sugar exporter, even in the global market. Today, consumers and investors around the world have begun to scrutinise the ethics of labour in supply chains. This pressure is visible from cocoa plantations to garment factories. It could come to bear on Indian sugar as well.

So what is the solution? The first thing is to stop viewing this problem as "women's wrong decision". The woman who makes the decision is under pressure imposed by the system. Responsibility therefore falls on the factories, the mukadams, the private hospitals and the government. The demand to bring sugarcane cutting under a separate law and to make a written agreement between factory owners, mukadams and workers mandatory is an old one. It has been raised in the Legislative Council too. The agreement must guarantee relief during menstruation, sick leave and maternity leave, minimum wages, interest-free advances and safe housing. The government has hinted at considering a separate compensation scheme so that women do not lose their earnings. If it is actually implemented, there will be no reason left to remove the womb in order to save wages on period days. That would strike at the root of the problem.

The second thing is vigilance and accountability in the health system. We must check whether the district-level monitoring committees mentioned by the Supreme Court are actually working rather than existing only on paper. The medical reason for every surgery on a woman under forty, and the name of the doctor who advised it, should be recorded. Private hospitals should be audited for such surgeries, and claims under insurance schemes should be examined. In the hospitals where the 107 Jalna surgeries took place, it must be investigated whether every patient was told about alternative treatments before surgery, whether written consent was taken, and whether the consent was truly understood. At the village level, ASHA workers and health workers should be made responsible for following up with women during the cane-cutting migration. Just like seasonal hostels, providing mobile health teams, toilets, water and free sanitary pads in the fields is not expensive; it can be done quickly and at low cost. Some women workers have themselves become health ambassadors and guided fellow women in experiments of this kind. Such experiments should be expanded.

The third thing is awareness and education. The misconception that surgery is the final answer to period troubles must be dispelled. Women must be told, in their own language and by people they trust, that medication and less invasive options exist for period pain, heavy bleeding or fibroids. We must also break the chain in which girls' education stops once their periods begin and they are married early. If education and alternative employment are available, the next generation will not inherit the sickle and the advance as a legacy of debt.

Finally, this question is a moral test of our society. We use sugar in our tea, in our sweets and in the sweetness of our festivals. If the price of that sugar is being paid by a woman's body, can we stomach that sweetness? The story of the 107 women of Jalna asks us this question again. In 2019, some steps were taken in response to the call that came from Beed, but they proved inadequate. Now the same warning has come once more. This time, concrete and concerted work is needed at four levels together: investigation, law, compensation and basic facilities in the fields. Menstruation is a natural process of the body. The minimum that any civilised society must ensure is that no one loses her livelihood because of it, and that no one is forced to cut her own body out of fear of it.
